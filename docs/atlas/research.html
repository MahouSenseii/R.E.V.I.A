WHERE IT STANDS / 13 NOVEMBER 2026

What RNL can and cannot do today.

Plain statements, each resting on a study whose scripts are published. Both studies are small and use random facts.

SHOWN SO FAR

  • A routed model answered 100.0% with 26.7% of its parameters loaded for a one-topic conversation; an ordinary model the size of that loaded part answered 63.8% (STATE-020).
  • Twice the knowledge with twice the experts left the loaded part the same size, at 91.8% (STATE-020).
  • With no cue words and no turn markers, a small planner found the topic by itself, and the routed model matched a fully loaded one, 97.7% against 97.5%, with 37% loaded (STATE-021).
  • It knows when it is guessing: 99.6% sure on a clear first question, 67% sure when the name fits two topics (STATE-021).

NOT SHOWN YET

  • Real language. Both studies use random facts on a model of about 2 million parameters.
  • Real memory or speed savings. Parameters were counted as needed; nothing was unloaded and no response time was measured.
  • Better answers. The routed model ties a fully loaded model of the same size, and conventional every-token routing reached that level on two seeds of three, with everything loaded.
  • Learning over time, or pathways reused across skills.
  • An independent rerun. The scripts are public; nobody else has run them yet.

01 / LATEST LOCAL STUDIES

What the newer studies found.

Positive, negative and stopping results are shown together. Detailed methods, frozen artifacts, checkpoints and traces for STATE-011–019 remain local and unpublished. From STATE-020 each study’s scripts, summaries and reproduction steps are published with it.

STATE-021Cue-free routing · small trained model

The model found the topic by itself and matched the fully loaded model with 37% loaded.

No cue words and no turn markers. A small planner that learns only by predicting the upcoming words found all 16 topics. The routed model scored 97.7% with 37% of its parameters loaded; a fully loaded ordinary model 97.5%; conventional every-token routing reached 96–97% on two seeds and stalled at 69% on the third, with 99.9% loaded. It does not answer better; it loads less.

Detail & evidence scope

STATE-021, 13 November 2026. 16 topics; a name is two syllables and fits 1.88 topics on average, so the topic has to be worked out from the names asked so far and can change without notice; 196,608 random facts; seeds 11–13, 24,000 steps. The planner is a separate 78,000-parameter reader trained first, alone, to predict the next 8 tokens through one of 16 guesses; it sees no topic label and no answer, and the route is a fixed function of its guess. One topic, one unannounced switch, a change every two questions: routed 97.7%, 95.4%, 82.0% with 36.6%, 47.9%, 60.9% loaded; ordinary fully loaded 97.5%, 95.3%, 83.6%; every-token routing 97.3%, 68.8% and 96.4% on its three seeds for one topic; ordinary models of 0.74M and 0.55M 67.6% and 61.1%. The best any reader could do is 67.4% on a first question, 96.9% on the second and 99.7% on the third; the routed model scored 66.8%, 96.3% and 99.8%. On a first question whose name fits one topic it was right 100% and 99.6% sure; with two possible topics, right 48.5% and 67% sure.

How to reproduce
  • Needs Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA and one NVIDIA card. There is no data to fetch: the task is generated from a fixed seed.
  • Headline comparison: run run_find.py with --arm guess, --arm dense and --arm fast, each with --steps 24000 for seeds 11, 12 and 13; then summarize.py on the folder, and ceiling.py for the best-possible figures. run_all.sh runs every reported run.
  • Expect the routed model at 97.6–97.7% with about 36.6% of its parameters loaded for a one-topic conversation, the ordinary model at 97.5–97.6%, and each planner log to say 16 of 16 guesses in use. Last digits can differ between graphics cards.
  • About 12 minutes a run on an RTX 5070.
  • Scripts, summaries and the full guide: research/rnl/state-021 at 648e8cb ↗

Scripts published · toy model, random facts · parameters counted as needed, not actually unloaded · three seeds; planner variants on one pilot seed

STATE-020First routing test · small trained model

With a quarter of its parameters loaded, a routed model matched the whole one.

Planning the route once per turn, a small model needed 26.7% of its parameters for a one-topic conversation and answered 100.0%. An ordinary model the size of that loaded part, trained equally long, answered 63.8%. Routing every token kept everything loaded.

Detail & evidence scope

STATE-020, 13 November 2026, the first test of the owner’s idea for RNL: only the parameters on a route are loaded, and slow signals pick the route. A 1.88M-parameter transformer with 16 experts per block, 2 in use; 200,704 random facts in 16 topics, each turn opening with its topic’s cue words; seeds 11–13, settings tuned on a separate seed. A planner reads the opening words and holds the route for the turn. At 24,000 steps: 100.0% on every seed with 26.7% loaded for one topic, 36.3% across one switch and 48.2% when taking turns; an ordinary 0.50M model gave 63.8% (61.7–66.2). At 6,000 steps: 90.3% (88.3–92.6), the whole ordinary model 96.2%, the small one 32.2%. With 32 topics and 32 experts the loaded part stayed 0.52M (14.9%) at 91.8%; an ordinary 1.89M model gave 74.2%, and the routed model left at 16 experts 67–73% (two seeds). A topic change brings in about 5.5 experts; this PC copies 6–13 GB a second onto its cards. The router’s own weights needed no training: what was learned is that different openings of one topic give the same route.

How to reproduce
  • Needs Python 3.12, PyTorch 2.11 with CUDA and one NVIDIA card. There is no data to fetch: the task is generated from a fixed seed.
  • Headline comparison: run run_routes.py with --arm turn --noise 0 --steps 24000, and with --arm dense --experts 2 --steps 24000, each for seeds 11, 12 and 13; then summarize.py on the folder. run_all.sh runs all 59 runs of the study.
  • Expect the routed model at 100.0% on every seed with 26.7% of its parameters loaded for a one-topic conversation, and the ordinary 0.50M model at 61.7–66.2%. Last digits can differ between graphics cards.
  • About 17 minutes for a 24,000-step run on an RTX 5070; under an hour and a half for the headline comparison.
  • Scripts, summaries and the full guide: research/rnl/state-020 at 92d50e8 ↗

Scripts published · toy model, random facts · topics announced by cue words, turn starts given · parameters counted as needed, not actually unloaded · three seeds

STATE-019Baseline · no training

On longer histories the store held and context memory fell.

At 128–384 facts (3,500–10,400 tokens) the 0.8B with a store answered 86–91% and the 4B 92–97%. In context the 0.8B fell to 33–47%, the 4B to 42–69% and the 9B to 28–39%. Sixteen times more context added only 126–320 MiB of VRAM.

Detail & evidence scope

STATE-019, 13 November 2026. Same task, generator and scoring as STATE-018, on new development histories of 128, 256 and 384 facts; 8 histories and 64 questions a size; nothing trained, sealed test closed. Paired on the same 192 questions, the 0.8B with a store beat the 4B in context by 35.4 points (95% interval 28.6 to 41.7) and the 9B by 54.7 (49.0 to 60.9). In context the 0.8B gave old or wrong values and the 4B and 9B declined; exactly spelled names suffered too (0.8B 40–47%; 4B 59% and 9B 55% at 256 facts) against 94–100% with the store. VRAM in use after loading, at 1,024 and 16,384 context: 0.8B 677 and 803 MiB, 4B 2,851 and 3,171, 9B 5,083 and 5,385. The saving comes from using a smaller model, not a shorter context. Filing took 0.25 s a note on the 0.8B and 0.40 s on the 4B at every size (3.6 and 5.9 minutes for one 384-fact history); about 2% of notes were filed unusably. Aliases stay the store’s weak spot: 56–62% on the 0.8B, 67–85% on the 4B.

Local study · development names only · 64 questions a cell, one run · synthetic task · the longest history still fits the context window · 9B with a store not run

Next Still a baseline, not a new structure. A trained part has to beat about 86–91% at 677 MiB; the room is in aliases and in filing on the 0.8B. No such training has been run; the routing idea is tested in STATE-020.

STATE-018Baseline · no training

A small model with a memory store beat a large one that held everything in context.

Qwen3.5-0.8B filing notes into a store answered 88.6% of questions in 685 MiB of VRAM; the 9B with the whole history in its context answered 44.7% in 5,480 MiB. With the store, the 4B and 9B reached 94.7% and 94.5%.

Detail & evidence scope

STATE-018, 12 November 2026: the two plain ways a local model can remember, so a new structure has a number to beat. STATE-017’s people-and-facts task with updates, corrections, misspelled names and aliases; 80 development histories of 8–64 facts, 640 questions; nothing trained, sealed test closed. In context: 0.8B 69.5%, 4B 59.8%, 9B 44.7%. Filing each note into the keyed store, one model call per note: 88.6%, 94.7%, 94.5%; a rule-based extractor with the same store scored 640/640. In context the 0.8B gave an old value 112 times; the 4B and 9B declined 242 and 346 times, mostly on misspelled names and aliases. The store’s remaining errors were the model’s filing: aliases 55–82% correct, 37–120 notes per run filed unusably. Filing cost 23–57 minutes against 1–5 to answer from context. Telling the in-context models that a name may be misspelled or a nickname (STATE-018b) gave 69.4%, 71.7% and 46.1%, still below the smallest model with a store.

Local study · development data only · one prompt per arm, one run · synthetic task

HYBRID-CHECKStopping estimate

A learned name matcher had little room over a good store.

With a fairly tuned fuzzy store, a perfect name matcher would add only about 2.5 points on harder misspellings, and no memory or speed saving was available.

Detail & evidence scope

Pre-check before any training: a learned name matcher in front of a replace-on-write store. On 400 new development histories with typos, truncations, dropped vowels, sound-alikes and swapped halves (tuned on half, scored on the other), the fuzzy store scored 97.5% with unlimited memory and 76.0% / 91.2% / 97.5% at 128 / 256 / 512 floats. A perfect matcher on the same stored names would score 100% / 78.7% / 93.6% / 100%, so the most any matcher could add was +2.3 to +2.8 points (paired 95% intervals within +1.6 to +3.6). A first baseline that skipped character similarity wrongly suggested 17–21 points of room; the corrected one is reported. Stored names cost about 2 floats each, so a learned key cannot save memory here.

Local estimate · development data only · no training · not proof that every hybrid is useless

Next Stopped for this design. Other stores, noise types or tasks could leave more room.

STATE-017Negative result

Two trained RNL designs fell short of a simple store.

On development data, RNL stayed 7–32 points below a compact keyed store at every memory size tested; a diagnosed layout fix closed only part of the gap.

Detail & evidence scope

STATE-017: a synthetic memory task with people’s facts, updates, corrections, misspellings, aliases and overload, every system reading the same text through one extractor. Budgets of 64–512 floats per conversation, three seeds each, CPU only. Run 1 (36 models) was 17–32 points below the matched store; ideal storage with its own learned addresses reached only 44–73%, so the layout, not the training, was the limit. Run 2 (27 models, rules frozen in advance) gave each attribute its own name-addressed block: +7–11 points over run 1, still 7–24 below the store on every seed, every paired interval below zero. Twice the training added under 1 point. Remaining failures: aliases (about 50% against 84% for exact names), many facts, older facts fading under later writes. The sealed test was never opened.

Local study · development data only · detailed source, checkpoints and traces unpublished

Next Closed under its stop rule. It does not show that RNL cannot work; other layouts, alias mechanisms, tasks and budgets are untested.

STATE-011-016Earlier local studies

Six earlier synthetic memory studies, kept as one entry.

A learned memory recalled 4,009 of 4,032 facts where a fresh GRU recalled 1,035 (STATE-011). It lost ground as unrelated writes were added, 998/1,008 then 970/1,008 (012), mostly on older facts (013, 014). A different reader made it worse, 963/1,008 (015); separate slots fixed it, 1,008/1,008 (016).

Detail & evidence scope

Synthetic recall with four supplied keys and eight values, three seeds. STATE-011 compared unequal recipes and showed no speed advantage. STATE-012 to 014 are inference-only audits of saved models: older facts became wrong after later unrelated writes (10/504 after four writes, 32/504 after sixteen). STATE-015 changed only the reader: 963/1,008 against 970/1,008. STATE-016 used fixed separate slots: 1,008/1,008 against 970/1,008, 38 rescues and no new errors. None establishes general routing, language understanding or a benefit to Revia. The six full cards are in the Atlas at commit 6c72d7d.

What a reproducible, reviewed handoff needs
  • Exact method/config/source/test deltas and all positive and negative results.
  • Fixture generators, seeds, evaluation definitions and per-example predictions/traces.
  • Three frozen delta snapshots, tensor inventory, original hashes and lineage.
  • The comparison fixture/parser, 32 retained requests/responses and completed 46-output seal.
  • An inventory mapping symbolic artifact IDs to sanitized filenames and export hashes.

Raw local logs/manifests can contain private paths and process metadata. The exact export inventory and destination need review before any upload. A summary alone does not reproduce these studies.
